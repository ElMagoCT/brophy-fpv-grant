01 Where we are today

We didn't wait for the money. Tier 0 is already running.

  • Five FPV simulators in the IC, open to anyone, 7:00–4:00 on school days.
  • A kiosk students built: tap your name, pick a sim, fly. Hours log themselves.
  • Ground School lessons and quizzes run on the same machine while someone else has the seat.
  • A public website shows the lab's hours, the leaderboard and the whole badge path.the numbers on the right are live from the lab, not from a slide.

02 The problem

Every year a club like this restarts from zero.

  • Skills leave with the seniors. Whoever knew how to build, tune and fly graduates, and nothing is written down.
  • Nobody can say who may fly what. Expensive gear with no rule for who is trained to touch it.
  • FPV looks like a toy until it isn't. LiPo fires, flyaways and controlled airspace are real, and there is no safety standard on paper.
  • Students who want to film for the school have no path to get there.
what a grant usually buys

Drones. They get flown by the two people who already knew how, and when those two graduate the drones sit in a drawer.

what we are asking for instead

A training fleet for a certification system. The drones in the budget are the hardware the badge program runs on, and the program is what the school keeps.

03 The idea

Badges unlock gear.

  • A badge is one specific, testable skill, signed off by a certified member.
  • Earn every badge in a tier and the gate opens: the next drone is yours to fly.
  • Four tiers: Simulator → Tiny Whoop → Full-size → Event Pilot.
  • The expensive gear is never in untrained hands, and "who may fly what" is written down, not remembered.
  • this is a live rendering of the club's real badge catalogue, watching one pilot climb.
04 The curriculum tap any badge

28 badges. Four tracks. Every one has a "why".

05 How a badge is earned

Demonstration, not attendance.

  • Quiz badges: short lessons, then a 10-question quiz. 80% passes, retake any time, the kiosk awards it by itself.
  • Bench badges: a step-by-step checklist on the kiosk, a photo of the work, an instructor approves.
  • Witnessed badges: an examiner watches you perform the standard written on the card.
  • Official: the free FAA TRUST certificate before anyone touches a real drone.
  • students run every sign-off. Nothing here costs the school anything beyond the training fleet.
06 Teaching

The classroom is already built.

  • 45 lessons, about 140 minutes, one module per knowledge badge: safety, batteries, electronics, radio, airspace, emergencies, event ops.
  • Works on the kiosk and on any phone. Lessons, drills and quizzes; progress follows the pilot.
  • Stick drills on the real transmitter: hover trainer, orientation recovery, the first flip and roll.
  • Written by students, maintained by students.a new member has something to do on day one, with or without a free seat.

07 Why sim first

Crashes should cost nothing while you learn.

  • 7.5 hours in the simulator before the first real flight. Acro muscle memory with zero risk.
  • Proficient Flight is sim-only on purpose: orbit, Split-S, gaps, a finished race. It gates the first real battery.
  • Line of Sight: if the video cuts out, the pilot can still level out and land.
  • Then a sub-250 g whoop that can't do much damage. Full-size only after Freestyle, Electrical Components, Radio Protocol and Field Repair.
  • every gate is placed where the next mistake starts to cost real money or real safety.

08 Sustainability

Built to outlive its founders.

  • Mentor badge: coach a new member through their first two badges. The program trains its own replacements.
  • Examiner badge: who may sign off is defined by badges held plus officer approval, not by who is loudest.
  • Fleet Steward: check-in/out and a maintenance log for school-funded gear, one semester at a time.
  • Everything is written down: the safety rules, the badge sheet, seven printed posters, a website that runs itself.
  • Every award is recorded and signed by the approving instructor, and progress only moves forward.
  • Gear is bought to last: goggles and radios about 8 years, airframes about 4. A one-time build-out, not a recurring ask.

09 For the students

What a student walks away with.

  • Hands: soldering, electronics, firmware, field repair. Skills that transfer straight to robotics and engineering.
  • Judgment: airspace law near Sky Harbor, FAA TRUST, emergency procedures, battery safety. Responsibility, not just reflexes.
  • Professionalism: writing a shot list with a coach, briefing bystanders, running a flight zone at a game.
  • Teaching: the path ends with coaching the next class, then examining them.
  • A record: a logged, visible badge board that says exactly what they can do.

10 For Brophy

What Brophy gets.

a standing program

A safety standard and a clear answer to "who may fly what"

  • Written rules, a published badge sheet, a signed record of every award
  • FAA TRUST for every flying member, free
  • Nothing over 250 g until the top tier
a pipeline

Trained, certified pilots every single year

  • New members start on Simulator Flight the week they join
  • Mentors and examiners are produced by the path itself
  • Fifteen joint community-period meetings with RC Club a year
the output

Footage and moments the school can actually use

  • Best of Brophy: short aerial clips any team or club can use, two showcase films a year
  • A live FPV race at the pep rally, pilot's-eye view on the scoreboard
  • A recruiting moment: students see FPV before they hear about it

A model other clubs can copy. Badge → gate → gear is not drone-specific. Robotics, shop, A/V: any club with expensive tools and a safety question can run the same system.

11 The equipment, briefly

The drones are the training fleet the badge system runs on.

trainers · tier 1

Two Meteor 75 Pro tiny whoops

Sub-250 g, ducted props, safe indoors, cheap to crash. Eight students are training today without a drone to share. Two trainers means two first flights at once.

pilot kit

Two goggle-and-radio sets

Two pilots can train or fly at the same time, on the sim or in the air. Goggles and radios last about eight years, so this is the part the school buys once.

camera ship · tier 2

One Pavo 20 Pro cinewhoop

Ducted, so it can fly near people; a D-log camera, so the footage can be graded. The drone Best of Brophy is shot on, and the airframe the top-tier badges are tested on.

keep it flying

Spares, batteries, a smoke stopper, two screwdrivers

Members fix what they break: that is a badge, not a chore. Spare frames, props and motors mean a crash is a repair lesson, not a purchase order.

About $1,975 estimated, inside the $2,000 ask, with the pep-rally course priced after Stuco confirms the slot. Many essentials are donated by members. The full parts list, with a reason beside every line, is in the proposal.

12 The ask

$2,000, tied to three projects.

  • The Badge Program: a standing certification path, run inside every club meeting.
  • Best of Brophy: aerial clips through the year, a showcase film each semester.
  • The pep-rally race: a live FPV race on the scoreboard, if Stuco and Student Activities approve the segment.
  • Next steps: Mr. Reasy, then Mr. Burr for Student Activities sign-off, Stuco on the rally slot, then the fall assembly pitch with recruiting.
  • Once funded: order the fleet, publish the badge sheet, certify current pilots as examiners, start every new member on Simulator Flight.
